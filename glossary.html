Zero Trust Principles 6 terms
Zero Trust Architecture★ KEY CONCEPT
A security model where no one is automatically trusted — not even people already inside the company's network. Every single login and access request must be verified, every time, no matter who you are or where you're connecting from.
Everyday analogy
Imagine a hospital where every door requires your ID badge — even if you work there. The fact that you got through the front door doesn't automatically let you into the pharmacy, the operating theatre, or the records room. You must prove you have a reason to be in each specific place, every time.
Why it matters
Old security models assumed anyone inside the office network was trustworthy. But modern attackers can get inside the network — so zero trust assumes the attacker might already be in and verifies everything anyway.
Least Privilege★ KEY CONCEPT
Every user or system should only have access to the exact things they need to do their job — nothing more. A junior employee shouldn't be able to see the CEO's payroll. A delivery driver's app shouldn't have access to your contacts.
Everyday analogy
A new employee at a cinema is given a key to the staff entrance and the popcorn room — not the safe, the manager's office, or the CCTV control room. They get exactly what they need, nothing extra.
Why it matters
If an account is hacked, least privilege limits the damage. A hacker who breaks into a low-level account can only access what that account can access — they can't automatically reach sensitive data.
Real example: The 2023 MOVEit breach affected 2,500+ organisations partly because service accounts had far more access than they needed. One over-privileged account gave attackers access to everything.
Assume BreachPRINCIPLE
A mindset where security teams plan as if attackers are already inside the system. Instead of only trying to keep attackers out, you also minimise what they can do if they're already in.
Everyday analogy
A bank doesn't just lock the front door — it also puts each deposit in a separate vault, uses dye packs, has silent alarms, and limits how much any one teller can access. Even if a robber gets in, the damage is limited.
Why it matters
No security is perfect. Attackers will sometimes get in. "Assume breach" means you've planned for that and built systems that limit the damage — rather than being caught completely off guard.
Defence in Depth★ KEY CONCEPT
Using multiple layers of security so that if one fails, others still protect you. Instead of relying on just a password, you also use MFA, device checks, network monitoring, and so on.
Everyday analogy
A medieval castle had a moat, then outer walls, then inner walls, then a keep, then a locked vault — each a separate barrier. Getting through one didn't mean you'd broken in. You had to beat every layer.
Why it matters
No single security measure is foolproof. Passwords can be guessed. MFA can sometimes be bypassed. But if an attacker has to defeat five different protections in a row, the chances of stopping them dramatically increase.
Continuous MonitoringPRINCIPLE
Security systems that watch what users do during their session, not just at login. If something unusual happens mid-session — like downloading 50,000 files — access can be revoked automatically.
Everyday analogy
Your bank doesn't just check your PIN when you tap your card — it also monitors your spending patterns in real time. If your card is used in two countries at the same time, it freezes automatically, even though you already "passed" the PIN check.
Why it matters
Getting past login doesn't mean a session should be trusted forever. An attacker who steals a valid session cookie could impersonate you after you've logged in. Continuous monitoring catches that.
Trust ScoreCONCEPT
A number calculated in real time that reflects how confident the system is that a login request is legitimate. It takes into account device health, location, MFA status, behaviour history, and risk signals.
Everyday analogy
Like a credit score, but for security. A high score means "everything checks out." A low score means "something's off — let's ask more questions or block access until we're sure."
Why it matters
It allows security systems to make nuanced decisions — instead of just "yes" or "no," they can say "yes, but we'll watch closely" or "we need you to verify again before we let you in."

Identity & Access Control 7 terms
Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)★ KEY CONCEPT
A login process that requires more than one piece of proof that you are who you say you are. The three factors are: something you know (password), something you have (a phone or token), and something you are (fingerprint/face).
Everyday analogy
Like withdrawing cash from an ATM: you need your card (something you have) AND your PIN (something you know). Stealing just the PIN isn't enough — you need both.
Why it matters
Microsoft found that MFA blocks 99.9% of automated password attacks. Even if someone steals your password, they still can't log in without the second factor.
Types of MFA (weakest → strongest): SMS text code → Authenticator app → Push notification → Hardware key (YubiKey) → Passkey / biometric. SMS is better than nothing, but can be beaten by SIM swapping.
CredentialsBASICS
The information you use to prove your identity — most commonly a username and password. Credentials can also include biometrics, security keys, or certificates.
Everyday analogy
Your credentials are like a key to a house. If someone copies your key, they can get in without you knowing. The stronger the lock (password complexity + MFA), the harder it is to copy.
Why it matters
Stolen credentials are the #1 way attackers get into systems. Billions of credentials have been leaked in data breaches — and many people reuse the same password everywhere, so one breach can unlock dozens of accounts.
Privilege Levels★ KEY CONCEPT
Different tiers of permission that determine what a user is allowed to access. A Level 1 intern can only see basic documents. A Level 4 admin can access everything. Higher privilege = more responsibility and more risk if compromised.
Everyday analogy
Like a government security clearance — Confidential, Secret, Top Secret. The higher your clearance, the more sensitive information you're allowed to see. Not everyone gets Top Secret just because they work for the government.
Why it matters
If a low-privilege account is hacked, only low-privilege data is at risk. High-privilege accounts are "crown jewels" for attackers — compromising an admin account can give access to everything.
Privilege EscalationATTACK
When someone (or a piece of malware) gains higher-level access than they were originally given — for example, a normal user account gaining admin rights by exploiting a software bug.
Everyday analogy
You start as a guest at a hotel and find an unattended master key — suddenly you can enter every room. You didn't earn that access, you exploited a gap in security to take it.
Why it matters
Most attacks follow a path: get low-level access, escalate to admin, then cause maximum damage. Blocking privilege escalation stops attackers from moving beyond their initial foothold.
Real example: Dave's dossier — he ran a privilege escalation script on his work laptop, attempting to access the Production DB from a Level 1 account. This pattern matches known attacker playbooks.
Shared CredentialsBAD PRACTICE
When multiple people share the same username and password to access a system. This makes it impossible to know which individual performed any specific action.
Everyday analogy
Imagine if five colleagues shared one key to the office and something went missing. You know the key was used — but you have no idea which of the five people actually used it. The audit trail is useless.
Why it matters
Accountability is a core security principle. If something goes wrong, you need to know exactly who did what. Shared credentials make this impossible, and also increase the chance of the password leaking.
Stale CredentialsRISK
Login details that still work even though they shouldn't — for example, an ex-employee whose account was never disabled after they left the company.
Everyday analogy
Like a hotel that forgets to deactivate a guest's key card after checkout. Weeks later, the ex-guest still has a working key to their old room.
Why it matters
One study found that 58% of ex-employees still had active access to company systems after leaving. Attackers actively look for stale accounts because they often go unmonitored.
In the dossier: The TechVendor account — a former support agent left but their shared credentials were never changed. The account was later used from a TOR exit node at 3am on a Sunday.
Session TokenCONCEPT
A temporary digital pass your browser stores after you log in, so you don't have to enter your password on every page. It usually expires after a set time. If stolen, an attacker can use it to access your account without needing your password.
Everyday analogy
Like a wristband at a theme park — once you've paid and gone through the entrance, the wristband lets you on rides without paying again. But if someone else puts on your wristband, they get free rides too.
Why it matters
Session hijacking — stealing someone's active session token — is a real attack. Zero trust systems expire tokens frequently and require re-verification for sensitive actions, even mid-session.

Attack Types 8 terms
Phishing★ KEY CONCEPT
A type of scam where an attacker sends a fake email or message pretending to be a trusted source (your bank, your employer, Netflix) to trick you into clicking a link and entering your login details on a fake website.
Everyday analogy
Someone calls you pretending to be your bank, says there's been suspicious activity, and asks you to "confirm" your card number and PIN. The bank would never call and ask for this — but it works on thousands of people every day.
Why it matters
Phishing is the #1 cause of data breaches worldwide. It doesn't matter how strong your password is if you hand it to an attacker on a fake login page.
In the dossier: Carol clicked a link from a fake HR email and partially entered her credentials before noticing the URL was wrong. Even partial entry can give attackers a foothold.
Credential Stuffing★ KEY CONCEPT
An automated attack where hackers take stolen username/password pairs from one breach and try them on other websites. If you used the same password on Netflix and your work email, and Netflix was breached, attackers will try that password on your work email too.
Everyday analogy
Imagine someone found a master key that works on one type of lock. They walk down every street trying it on every door of the same lock type. They don't know which doors it'll open, but they try them all — automatically.
Why it matters
There are literally billions of stolen credentials available online for free. Using unique passwords for every account is the single best defence. A password manager makes this easy.
Impossible Travel★ KEY CONCEPT
A security alert triggered when the same account logs in from two locations that are too far apart too quickly — for example, London at 8am and Lagos at 8:22am. Since no plane can cover that distance in 22 minutes, one of those logins cannot be the real user.
Everyday analogy
Your Oyster card taps at London Bridge station at 9:02am and then at Heathrow airport at 9:03am. The tube journey takes 45 minutes — so one of those taps must be someone using a cloned card.
Why it matters
This signal is a near-certain indicator of account compromise. The physics of travel make it one of the hardest signals for attackers to fake.
SIM Swap AttackATTACK
When an attacker convinces a mobile phone carrier to transfer your phone number to a new SIM card they control. After the swap, all SMS messages — including MFA codes — go to the attacker, not you.
Everyday analogy
An attacker calls your mobile network pretending to be you, says they got a new phone, and asks to transfer the number. If the carrier doesn't verify properly, your number — and every text sent to it — is now theirs.
Why it matters
This is why SMS-based MFA is weaker than an authenticator app. The app code is generated on your physical device — transferring your phone number doesn't help the attacker if the code isn't sent by text.
Social EngineeringATTACK
Manipulating people — rather than hacking systems — into giving up information or access. Phishing is one type, but social engineering also includes impersonating a colleague, creating a fake emergency, or building false trust over time.
Everyday analogy
A con artist doesn't pick your lock — they knock on your door dressed as a plumber, tell you there's an urgent leak, and you let them in. The technical security was bypassed completely by tricking the human.
Why it matters
Humans are often the weakest link in security. No software can protect against a person being tricked. Security awareness training — knowing what these attacks look like — is the best defence.
Data BreachCONCEPT
When private or sensitive information is accessed or stolen without permission. This could be millions of passwords from a hacked website, medical records from a hospital, or financial data from a retail company.
Everyday analogy
Imagine a library with millions of people's private diaries stored in it. A thief breaks in and photocopies all of them. The diaries are now out in the world — and you can't un-leak them.
Why it matters
Over 33 billion records were stolen in data breaches in 2023 alone. Once your data is out, it stays out — it can appear on dark web marketplaces and be used in attacks for years afterward.
Check yourself: Visit haveibeenpwned.com and enter your email address (or a parent's, with permission). It shows which breaches have included that email — this is a legitimate, safe tool used by security professionals.
TyposquattingTECHNIQUE
Registering a domain name very similar to a real one to trick people into visiting it. For example, techcorp-hr-portal.com instead of techcorp.com, or paypa1.com (with a 1) instead of paypal.com.
Everyday analogy
Someone opens a fake shop called "Nikee" next to a real Nike store, with a very similar logo and layout. Shoppers who don't look closely might walk in thinking it's the real thing.
Why it matters
Always check the full URL before entering passwords. The padlock icon just means the connection is encrypted — it does NOT mean the site is legitimate. Criminals can get padlocks for fake sites too.
Insider ThreatRISK
A security risk that comes from within the organisation — a current or former employee, contractor, or partner who misuses their legitimate access, either deliberately or accidentally.
Everyday analogy
A bank employee who has access to the vault every day, but one day decides to take money home. The vault's security was designed to keep outsiders out — it can't easily stop someone who already has the key.
Why it matters
Insider threats are hard to detect because the person is using legitimate access. Monitoring for unusual patterns — accessing files they've never touched, downloading large amounts of data — is key to catching them.
NET
Devices & Networks 6 terms
VPN (Virtual Private Network)★ KEY CONCEPT
A service that encrypts your internet connection and routes it through a server in another location. Companies use VPNs so employees working from home can securely connect to internal systems as if they were in the office.
Everyday analogy
Like sending your letter through a secure, sealed pneumatic tube instead of the regular post. Even if someone intercepts it mid-journey, they can't read the contents — it's encrypted. And it comes out at the company's private office, not the public post box.
Why it matters
Without a VPN, data sent over public wifi (e.g. a café) can be intercepted. A VPN makes that much harder. However, VPNs don't make you anonymous — your company can still see everything you do on their network.
TOR NetworkHIGH RISK
A network that hides your location and identity by bouncing your connection through multiple volunteer computers worldwide. Legitimate uses include journalism and activism in censored countries — but it is also widely used by criminals to hide their tracks.
Everyday analogy
Like passing a letter through 10 different anonymous postboxes in different countries, each one sealed and re-addressed, so nobody along the chain can see where it started or where it's going.
Why it matters
When a corporate login attempt comes from a TOR exit node, it almost always means the person is deliberately hiding their real location. Zero trust blocks this because accountability is impossible — a core requirement.
MDM / Device Management★ KEY CONCEPT
Software that lets a company monitor, manage, and enforce security policies on devices used for work. An MDM-enrolled device can be remotely wiped if lost, and must meet requirements like disk encryption and updated software before it can access company resources.
Everyday analogy
Like a company car — you can drive it, but the company knows where it is, can set speed limits, ensures it's properly maintained, and can immobilise it remotely if it's stolen.
Why it matters
An unmanaged personal device is a blind spot — the company has no idea if it's infected with malware. In zero trust, device posture is a mandatory signal before access is granted.
BYOD (Bring Your Own Device)RISK
A policy where employees use their personal laptops or phones for work instead of company-issued devices. Convenient — but it means the company can't fully control the security of those devices.
Everyday analogy
A surgeon bringing their own scalpels from home instead of using hospital-sterilised ones. The hospital has no way to guarantee those scalpels are clean or safe — they just have to trust the surgeon.
Why it matters
Personal devices often have outdated software, no enterprise antivirus, and might be shared with family members. Corporate data accessed on a personal device might be copied to personal cloud storage without the company knowing.
FirewallBASICS
A security system that monitors and controls incoming and outgoing network traffic based on predefined rules. It's like a security guard at the network gate — deciding what's allowed in and out.
Everyday analogy
Like a bouncer at a club who checks everyone coming in against a list of allowed and banned people. Anything that doesn't match the rules — blocked.
Why it matters
Firewalls are a foundational defence-in-depth layer. They don't solve everything — attackers can work around them — but they block a huge amount of automated scanning and malicious traffic from ever reaching your systems.
IP AddressBASICS
A unique number assigned to every device connected to the internet — like a postal address for your computer. It can be used to roughly identify your location and is used in impossible travel detection.
Everyday analogy
Like a postcode — it tells you roughly where someone is (city or region) but not their exact house. Security systems use IP addresses to flag unusual login locations.
Why it matters
Security logs record the IP address of every login attempt. This is how impossible travel is detected, how attack source countries are identified, and how TOR exit nodes are spotted — their IP addresses are published in public lists.

Security Tools & Systems 5 terms
SIEMTOOL
Stands for Security Information and Event Management. A system that collects and analyses logs from across all company systems in one place, looking for patterns that suggest a security incident — like 847 login failures in 4 minutes.
Everyday analogy
Like a security operations centre in a large shopping centre that receives feeds from every single camera and alarm — all displayed in one place so a small team can monitor everything at once and spot anything unusual.
Why it matters
Attacks rarely happen in isolation — they leave traces across multiple systems. A SIEM connects the dots between a failed login here, a suspicious file access there, and an unusual outbound connection somewhere else.
EDR (Endpoint Detection & Response)TOOL
Advanced security software installed on devices (endpoints) that continuously monitors behaviour — not just known viruses, but suspicious actions like a script trying to gain admin access or an app reading files it shouldn't.
Everyday analogy
Like a CCTV camera inside your house that doesn't just check who comes in the door, but watches everything that happens inside — and alerts security if someone starts trying to open the safe.
Why it matters
Traditional antivirus matches known malware signatures. EDR looks for suspicious behaviour — it can catch brand new attacks that have never been seen before, because it flags "this is weird" rather than "this matches a known virus."
HaveIBeenPwnedFREE TOOL
A free, legitimate website (haveibeenpwned.com) where you can check if your email address or phone number has appeared in any publicly known data breaches. Used by security professionals worldwide.
Everyday analogy
Like checking a public registry to see if your passport details have appeared on a stolen documents list. It doesn't give anyone access to your account — it just tells you if your information is already out there.
Why it matters
If your email has been in a breach, change the password for that service immediately — and change it on any other site where you used the same password. Companies like Microsoft and 1Password integrate with this service to automatically warn users.
Try it: Visit haveibeenpwned.com — it is completely safe and does not require you to enter a password. Just enter an email address to see its breach history.
Password ManagerBEST PRACTICE
An app that generates and stores a unique, strong password for every website you use. You only need to remember one master password, and the app handles the rest. Examples: Bitwarden (free), 1Password, Apple Keychain.
Everyday analogy
Like a keychain that holds a hundred unique, unpickable keys — one for every door you need to open. You don't need to remember them all; you just carry the keychain. You only need the one key to open the keychain itself.
Why it matters
Most people reuse passwords because they can't remember unique ones for 50 different sites. A password manager removes that excuse — every site gets a random 20-character password, so one breach doesn't compromise everything else.
EncryptionBASICS
The process of scrambling data so only someone with the correct key can read it. HTTPS on websites, end-to-end encryption on messaging apps, and disk encryption on laptops all use this principle.
Everyday analogy
Like writing a letter in a secret code that only the recipient knows how to decode. Even if a stranger intercepts the letter, they just see gibberish.
Why it matters
Without disk encryption, a stolen laptop's data can be read by anyone — even without the login password — by booting from a USB stick. With encryption, the data is unreadable without the decryption key.
DAT
Data & Privacy 4 terms
Audit TrailCONCEPT
A recorded log of every action taken in a system — who logged in, when, from where, what files they accessed, and what they changed. It's the paper trail that security teams use to investigate incidents.
Everyday analogy
Like a visitor sign-in book at a school reception — every person who enters writes their name, time in, and who they're visiting. If something goes wrong, the book shows exactly who was there.
Why it matters
After a breach, security teams forensically examine audit logs to understand exactly what happened and when. Shared credentials destroy the audit trail because you can't link actions to individuals.
Supply Chain RiskCONCEPT
The risk that comes from third-party suppliers or partners who have access to your systems. A company can have excellent internal security but be breached through a less-secure vendor who has a back-door connection.
Everyday analogy
You put a high-security lock on your front door — but you gave a spare key to a cleaning company whose staff leave their keys in their car. Your security is only as strong as the weakest person who has a key.
Why it matters
The SolarWinds attack (2020) compromised 18,000 organisations by hiding malware in a software update from a trusted supplier. The attack bypassed all internal security because it came from a trusted source.
Data HygienePRACTICE
The practice of keeping data clean, accurate, and limited to what's actually needed. This includes deleting old files, removing ex-employee accounts, and not storing sensitive data longer than necessary.
Everyday analogy
Like regularly clearing out old paperwork from your filing cabinet. Old bank statements from 1997 don't need to be kept forever — the longer you keep them, the longer someone who breaks in can access them.
Why it matters
In Dave's dossier, an old wiki page had server credentials from 2019 that were never deleted. Old data is often forgotten, unmonitored, and a goldmine for attackers. "You can't leak data you don't have."
CVE / VulnerabilityCONCEPT
A CVE (Common Vulnerability and Exposure) is a publicly disclosed security flaw in software. Every known vulnerability gets a unique ID (e.g. CVE-2024-21338) and a severity score. Software updates ("patches") fix these flaws.
Everyday analogy
Like a recalled car model with a known brake fault. The manufacturer publishes a recall notice (the CVE), and you need to take it to the garage to get it fixed (the patch). If you ignore the recall, you're driving a car with a known flaw attackers can exploit.
Why it matters
Bob's unpatched MacBook had 14 known CVEs. Attackers scan the internet looking for devices running unpatched software. Once found, they can exploit the known flaw to gain access — no guessing required.
Check it: Visit nvd.nist.gov and search any CVE number mentioned in the dossiers. You'll see the real severity rating, a technical description, and whether a patch exists.
Terms and definitions on this page are written for educational purposes and simplified for clarity. For precise technical definitions, refer to NIST SP 800-207, MITRE ATT&CK, and OWASP documentation.
